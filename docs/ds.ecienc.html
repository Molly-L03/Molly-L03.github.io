Ethical dilemma: AI recruitment and gender discrimination at Amazon

Author

Molly Liao

Published

November 25, 2026

In this project, we will explore an ethical dilemma in the data science context and answer a few questions inspired by the following two quotes from Data Feminism. The first asks about who and the second asks about why.

“[Examine] how power operates in the world today. This consists of asking who questions about data science: Who does the work (and who is pushed out)? Who benefits (and who is neglected or harmed)? Whose priorities get turned into products (and whose are overlooked)?”

“How did we get to the point where data science is used almost exclusively in the service of profit (for a few), surveillance (of the minoritized), and efficiency (amidst scarcity)?.”

The ethical dilemma I want to explore is whether historical applicant data should be used to train recruitment algorithms when that data reflects existing inequalities. In 2018, Reuters reported that Amazon developed an experimental recruitment tool trained on resumes submitted over a ten-year period. Because resumes predominantly came from men, the algorithm learned patterns that favoured male applicants and penalized terms associated with women. Although Amazon attempted to correct these biases, it could not ensure that the algorithm would not find other ways to discriminate, and eventually abandoned the project (Dastin, 2018).

What interests me most about this case is not simply that the algorithm was biased, but how the data used to train it contributed to that bias. The resumes reflected a historically male-dominated technology industry, yet Amazon used those patterns to evaluate future applicants. This raises the question of whether data that accurately reflects the past is necessarily appropriate for predicting the future. I am also interested in how applicants’ personal data is collected and whether they understand how it may be used beyond their individual job applications. I also examined the current Amazon privacy notice website to understand further how data could’ve been collected (Amazon, n.d.). As an international student, I find this especially concerning because I already face additional barriers in the job market, i.e. needing visa sponsorship. If an automated system were to treat sponsorship requirements or other characteristics of my background as undesirable, I could be excluded before anyone considered my actual qualifications. This case makes me question whose experiences are represented in training data, whose opportunities are overlooked, and who has the power to decide how applicants are evaluated.

1. Who was measured? Were they representative of the people to whom we want to generalize?

Amazon trained its algorithm using resumes submitted over a ten-year period. According to Reuters, most of these resumes came from men, reflecting the gender imbalance in the technology industry (Dastin, 2018). However, the algorithm was intended to evaluate future applicants, including women who were underrepresented in the industry back then. This is a great example of why having a large dataset does not necessarily mean having a more generalized or representative dataset: the dataset could be biased at the very beginning. The algorithm learned from who had applied in the past, rather than necessarily learning what makes someone qualified for a job. If women had fewer opportunities or were less represented in certain technical positions historically, the algorithm could reproduce that inequality by treating the patterns in men’s resumes as the standard for success.

2. How were the variables collected? Were they accurately recorded? Is there missing data?

I imagined the variables for the resume dataset simply be each word stripped from the resume when applicants submitted for the position. The algorithm then analyzed the words and learned the patterns. In this case, the variables are accurately recorded; however, Reuters reported that it favoured certain verbs that appeared more frequently on men’s resumes, such as “executed” and “captured,” while penalizing other terms associated with women (Dastin, 2018). Which links to the first question: a more diverse spectrum of data is missing. I do not think the words someone uses to describe their experiences necessarily reflect their actual abilities. Resume language can be influenced by cultural differences, confidence, access to career advice, how people have been socialized to describe their accomplishments, and, of course, gender. The problem is not necessarily that the data was recorded incorrectly, but that the algorithm may have interpreted the data in a way that does not accurately measure what the company actually cares about: a candidate’s ability to perform the job.

3. What is the permission and consent structure for using the data? Was it followed?

From Amazon’s Candidate Privacy Notice, it states that Amazon collects information such as resumes, demographic data, screening-question responses, work preferences, assessment results, and interview notes to manage the recruitment process. It also explains that candidate information may be retained after an unsuccessful application for purposes such as contacting applicants about future jobs, recordkeeping, and legal proceedings (Amazon, n.d.). While the notice explains the general collection and use of applicants’ personal data, it does not, in the version I reviewed, specifically explain that resumes would be used to train an experimental machine-learning recruitment model like the one described in the Reuters article. I cannot conclude from these two sources alone that Amazon violated applicants’ consent or privacy rights, especially because the privacy notice available today may not be the same as the notice applicants received when the experiment was developed. However, it raises an ethical question about whether applicants truly understand how their information might be used beyond evaluating their individual applications. Applying for a job requires sharing personal information, but that does not necessarily mean applicants expect their data to be repurposed to train algorithms that may influence future applicants’ opportunities. I think there should be greater transparency about how applicant data is used, particularly when it is being used to develop systems that could affect other people’s employment opportunities.

4. Recognize and mitigate bias in ourselves and in the data we use.

The Data Values and Principles manifesto emphasizes recognizing and mitigating bias in ourselves and in the data we use. Amazon’s case demonstrates that algorithms are not automatically objective just because they rely on mathematical calculations. The bias came partly from historical inequality in the technology industry, but also from the decision to use past resumes as a basis for predicting future candidates’ suitability. Amazon attempted to remove specific discriminatory patterns, such as penalizing the word “women’s,” but this did not guarantee that other forms of discrimination would disappear. I think developers need to consider not only whether a model performs accurately overall, but also who it performs poorly for and why. It is also important to have people with different backgrounds involved in developing and evaluating these systems, because people who are affected by potential discrimination may notice problems that developers themselves overlook. Amazon’s decision to abandon the tool then recognized that fixing a few biased variables was not enough to guarantee fairness.

Why does this matter?

Amazon’s recruitment tool illustrates how data science can reinforce existing power structures instead of challenging them. The company could benefit from faster and more efficient recruitment, but applicants could be harmed if they were ranked lower because of characteristics unrelated to their ability to do the job. For me, this issue is particularly concerning because job applicants often have very little control over how their applications are evaluated. We may not know whether AI is being used, what information it is analyzing, or why our applications are rejected. As an international student, I already face additional considerations when searching for employment, and I worry that an automated system could create another barrier that I cannot see or challenge. The problem is not simply that AI makes mistakes, but that those mistakes could be scaled across thousands of applicants while remaining invisible to the people affected. In the context of Data Feminism, this case makes me question whose priorities are being served by data science. If the primary goal is efficiency and reducing the cost of recruitment, fairness and individual circumstances may become secondary. I believe AI should support recruiters in evaluating candidates, rather than become an invisible gatekeeper that determines who gets the opportunity to be considered in the first place.
